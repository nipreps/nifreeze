    The global eddy model (GEM)¶

    NiFreeze can estimate the eddy currents of a DWI by registering each volume, on its own, to a prediction of it (--eddy). The global eddy model (GEM) estimates them for all the volumes at once instead, with a model of how the field of the eddy currents follows the diffusion gradient (GEM, #746). Its accuracy on the phantom is reported in GEM’s accuracy on the phantom.

    The model¶

    A field of eddy currents that is linear in position moves points only along the phase-encoding direction \(u\). Its mapping is \(E = I - u s^T\): a point \(x\) moves by \(-(s^T x)\, u\), where the three slopes \(s\) set how the displacement grows along each axis of the scanner. The field comes from the diffusion gradient, so its slopes follow the gradient. In volume \(v\), with the unit b-vector \(g_v\) and the b-value \(b_v\),

    \[s_v = \left(\frac{b_v}{b_\text{ref}}\right)^p C g_v,\]

    where the eddy-current tensor \(C\) (3 × 3) and the exponent \(p\) are the same for every volume (EddyTensor), and the reference b-value \(b_\text{ref}\) is the geometric mean of the b-values. \(C\) maps the gradient’s direction to the field’s slopes: with the scanner’s frame rotated by \(R\), it becomes \(R C R^T\), as a tensor does. The exponent is 1/2 when the field follows the gradient’s strength at a fixed timing, and 1 when it follows the b-value, as in the phantoms that TRXScan makes (Synthetic reference data). GEM estimates \(p\) between 0 and 2.

    The fields are zero at one point, by default the center of the field of view, the middle of its physical extent (field_of_view_centre()). A field that is zero at the point \(z\) also translates the volume along \(u\), by \(s^T z\). That translation is part of the head motion, by the convention of split_phase_encoding(), which splits a volume’s mapping into head motion and a field without translation. GEM derives each volume’s translation along \(u\) from its slopes and the zero point, and each volume keeps the rest of the head motion it is given.

    GEM comes in two forms:

    One tensor for all the volumes

    One \(C\) and one \(p\) (the default): 10 parameters, or 9 with the exponent held.

    One tensor for each shell

    One \(C\) for each segment of b-values, between the edges that the caller gives (SegmentedEddyTensor). The exponent is held, at 1/2 by default, and each segment’s reference b-value is the geometric mean of its b-values. On shelled data, every volume of a segment has the same b-value, so the exponent makes no difference.

    A stretch that every volume shares has no parameter of its own to go into, as long as the b-vectors average out. Fitted on the images, where the volumes weigh a little differently, a small part of it still reaches the fields.

    The b-vectors¶

    A GEM pass reads each volume’s b-vector in two frames: the scanner’s, for the field, and the head’s, for the target.

    The field follows the b-vector as acquired. The eddy currents come from the gradient the scanner plays, which stays put when the head moves. So \(g_v\) in the slopes above is the b-vector of the acquisition, in world (RAS+) coordinates, never rotated by the head motion. The pass gives fit() the dataset’s own gradient table, which keeps the b-vectors as they were read.

    The target follows the b-vector in the head. Each target predicts its volume as the head shows it at the reference position. When the head turns by \(Q_v\), the gradient \(g_v\) points along \(Q_v^{-1} g_v\) in the head, and the model predicts the volume there (realign_bvecs()). With the instant update (Design Principles), the realigned view that the model trains on holds each volume realigned and its b-vector rotated, together. Without it (--no-instant-update), the model trains on the original volumes, at the b-vectors as acquired. GEM derives only each volume’s translation along \(u\), and keeps the rotation of the head motion it is given, so the rotated b-vectors stay right through cascaded passes (Cascading passes).

    The outputs’ b-vectors turn with the head motion alone, as the view’s do: the eddy currents displace the image without turning the head (to_nifti()).

    How GEM is estimated¶

    Estimating GEM is one registration of all the volumes at once, with one set of parameters, in which each volume moves by its own field. The cost is the sum, over the volumes, of ITK’s similarity metric between each volume and its target, run in the stages of the itk section of AFFINE. ITK’s metric evaluates each volume’s similarity and its derivative, and scipy’s L-BFGS-B estimates the model’s parameters. The images are prepared as for ITK’s registration of each volume on its own: clipped to the volume’s intensity window, winsorized and histogram-matched as the settings ask.

    Running GEM¶

    GEM runs in the estimator’s eddy-current pass, given as its backend:

    from nifreeze.estimator import Estimator
from nifreeze.registration.gem import GEM

Estimator("gqi-krr", estimates="eddy", backend=GEM()).run(dwi)

    backend="gem" does the same: the estimator builds GEM for each run, with its defaults and the run’s align_config, clip and seed. The pass predicts every volume first, all from the same data, and adds each to the fit with its head motion; it then estimates the model and stores each volume’s head motion and field in the dataset. The fit takes the dataset’s phase-encoding direction, unless it is given its own (phase_encoding). One tensor for each shell takes the edges between the shells, for instance GEM(edges=(1500, 2750)) for shells at b=1000, 2000 and 3500 s/mm². zero_point sets where the fields are zero, in world (RAS+) coordinates, in mm, and exponent holds \(p\).

    On the command line, GEM is the default eddy-current correction of a DWI: three cascaded passes (Cascading passes), after the head-motion step, with the model of its cascade’s last level (gqi-krr by default) unless --eddy-model names another. --gem-passes sets the number of passes:

    nifreeze dwi.h5                        # head motion, then three passes of GEM
nifreeze dwi.h5 --gem-passes 1         # one pass
nifreeze dwi.h5 --gem-passes 0         # head motion only
nifreeze dwi.h5 --gem-passes 0 --eddy  # each volume registered on its own instead

    GEM needs the phase-encoding direction: the input’s BIDS sidecar or nfHDF5 file give it, or --phase-encoding. Without it, the default run estimates head motion only, with a warning. The command line fits GEM in its default form; its other options are set in Python.

    On DIPY’s Sherbrooke data, the default run took 17 min on six CPUs, against 5.4 min for head motion alone.

    tools/gem_sherbrooke.py makes both runs and the figure, on CPUs 0-5:

    taskset -c 0-5 python tools/gem_sherbrooke.py --work <work> --out docs/_static/gem

    The animations below play how the default run corrects each volume, on three axial, three coronal and three sagittal cuts. Each volume goes through five steps:

    1. the volume as found (original);

    2. a quiver of where GEM’s field sends its voxels, in the volume as acquired;

    3. the data travelling there (eddy-corrected);

    4. a quiver of where head motion sends them next;

    5. the data travelling into the reference (realigned).

    This is the order in which realignment undoes the two corrections. For each point of the reference, it reads the volume where head motion takes the point, moved along phase encoding by the field. So the field is undone first, in place, where the volume was acquired, and head motion after it. The third step’s frame is the volume read through the field alone, and the last one is the volume read once through both, as the command line writes it.

    On the phantom (Synthetic reference data), the copy with the largest head motion and eddy currents shows both corrections at work, against a known truth. Under the Sherbrooke protocol, every diffusion-weighted volume of this copy is turned by 4° and shifted by 4 mm, and its eddy currents move some voxel of the brain by 8 voxels (16 mm) along phase encoding (\(j-\)). The copy is noise-free, as simulated. The default run took 15 min on six CPUs.

    In the median volume, the estimated head motion moves the brain by 4.63 mm (the median over the brain), against a true 4.77 mm. The estimated eddy currents move it by 2.30 mm, against a true 2.56 mm. The corrected volume ends 1.67 mm from where the truth would put it: 1.30 mm at b=1000, 1.78 mm at b=2000 and 2.46 mm at b=3500. Four volumes, all at b=3500, end more than 5 mm away. In the worst, volume 181, head motion is estimated at 13.2 mm against a true 4.6 mm. Its solid outline does not fit the original volume, and its realigned cuts are off the reference. tools/gem_phantom.py makes the run and the animation:

    taskset -c 0-5 python tools/gem_phantom.py --dataset <phantoms> --work <work> \
    --out docs/_static/gem

    On DIPY’s Sherbrooke data, both corrections are far smaller:

    The dashed line outlines the brain mask at the reference position. The solid line is the mask moved by head motion and the eddy currents: it should fit the original volume, and it travels with the data into the reference. Arrows are drawn longer than the voxels move, by the factors in each legend. On the phantom, they are 2.5 times longer than the eddy currents move the voxels, and as long as head motion does. On the Sherbrooke data, they are 30 and 10 times longer. The data travel at true scale. A displacement across the cut is drawn as a circle of that size: ⊙ toward you, ⊗ away. With phase encoding along \(j\), GEM moves voxels only across the coronal cuts. In each cut’s corner, the b-vector is projected onto the cut. It turns with the head during the realignment, as the b-vectors the command line writes do. GEM’s field is drawn zero at the centre of the field of view, where GEM fits it, and the shift it makes there counts as head motion. Either way, the last frame is the volume the command line writes. Between the frames of each step, which are exact, the data move along the cut as the step moves it, while the part of the motion across the cut dissolves. Every volume plays in turn. The buttons below the animation, or the keys, change the speed (- and +), pause (space), go to the previous or next volume (arrow keys), and the bar jumps to any volume.

    The animation is drawn from the default run’s --write-hdf5 output:

    from nifreeze.data.dmri import DWI
from nifreeze.viz.corrections import animate_corrections

animate_corrections(DWI.from_filename("dwi.h5"), "corrections.svg")

    Each variant is one GEM, given as the pass’s backend:

    GEM()                                   # one C and one p, both estimated
GEM(exponent=0.5)                       # one C, with p held at 1/2
GEM(edges=(1500, 2750))                 # one C for each shell, p held at 1/2
GEM(fit_range=(0, 1500), exponent=0.5)  # C fitted at b=1000, given to all
GEM(zero_point=(0.0, -18.0, 12.0))      # the fields zero there (RAS+, mm)
GEM(phase_encoding="j-")                # its own phase-encoding direction

    The API reference of GEM lists the rest of its options.

    Cascading passes¶

    GEM’s passes can be cascaded, as many as needed: each pass is an estimator, and a chain of them (prev=) runs them in turn. Three passes, for instance:

    first = Estimator("gqi-krr", estimates="eddy", backend=GEM())
second = Estimator("gqi-krr", estimates="eddy", backend=GEM(), prev=first)
Estimator("gqi-krr", estimates="eddy", backend=GEM(), prev=second).run(dwi)

    Each pass’s model trains on the volumes that the previous pass corrected (the default); with run(dwi, instant_update=False), every pass repeats the first. FSL eddy, too, trains its predictions again on the corrected volumes at each of its iterations, five by default.

    Each pass after the first is given the head motion and the field that the pass before stored (#758). It factors that field out of each volume’s mapping, as a field zero at the zero point, and fits from the head motion that remains. Each volume’s translation along \(u\) then comes from the new field alone, however many passes came before. On the phantom, with gqi-krr’s predictions, each of the second and third passes recovers 59% to 77% of what the pass before left (What GEM recovers).

    Fitting on low b-values¶

    fit_range=(low, high) fits the model on the volumes whose b-value is at least low and below high only, and gives every volume its field from it (#757). For instance, GEM(fit_range=(0, 1500), exponent=0.5) fits \(C\) on the b=1000 shell. The reference b-value is then, by default, the geometric mean of the range’s b-values. With a single b-value in the range, the exponent cannot be estimated, and is held: at exponent, or at 1/2 with a warning. A wrong exponent goes straight to the other shells: held at 1/2 when the truth is 1, a fit on the b=1000 shell would give the b=3500 fields at 0.53 of their size. fit_range and edges exclude each other. On the phantom, at 16 mm, \(C\) fitted at b=1000 gives the smallest median field error in every pass (Fitting C at b=1000).

    The options combine: \(C\) fitted at b=1000, in three cascaded passes, for instance:

    chain = None
for _ in range(3):
    chain = Estimator(
        "gqi-krr",
        estimates="eddy",
        # p held at 1/2: give your scanner's, if known (the phantom's is 1)
        backend=GEM(fit_range=(0, 1500), exponent=0.5),
        prev=chain,
    )
chain.run(dwi)

    The antecedent: FSL eddy’s second-level model¶

    FSL’s eddy [1] estimates a field for each volume. With --slm linear (off by default), each of the field’s coefficients except its offset is pooled over the diffusion-weighted volumes of every shell, and replaced by its least-squares fit on \((b_v / 1000)\, g_v\), without an intercept. For the slopes, that is GEM with one tensor for all the volumes and \(p = 1\). eddy applies it in each iteration, after the step that updates each volume’s estimates. FSL recommends it for data with few directions, or whose directions do not cover the whole sphere. GEM differs from it in four ways:

    1. GEM is fitted on the images, in one registration of all the volumes, while eddy fits its model to the volumes’ own estimates, each counting the same.

    2. GEM estimates the exponent, or holds it at a value the caller gives; eddy holds it at 1.

    3. GEM’s zero point is given. By default, eddy regresses the sum of each volume’s field offset and translation along phase encoding on the gradients, across the volumes: the fit becomes the offset, and the rest the translation. In effect, its fields’ zero point is estimated.

    4. GEM can fit one tensor for each shell.

    [1]

    Jesper L. R. Andersson and Stamatios N. Sotiropoulos. An integrated approach to correction for off-resonance effects and subject movement in diffusion MR imaging. NeuroImage, 125:1063–1078, January 2016. doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2015.10.019.
